MODULE 02 · RICE, BEANS & GRAINS

The Cheap, Reliable Meal Base

Master starch and legumes and you are never more than 30 minutes and a few córdobas from a filling, complete meal. This is the highest-value everyday cooking you will ever learn — and it is almost entirely one variable: how much water goes into a starch granule, and how hard you make it work on the way in.

Month 2 High-value Gallo pinto
01

Why this matters

Proteins (Module 01) are the expensive, high-stakes part of a plate. Rice and beans are the opposite: pennies per serving, forgiving of timing, and — cooked properly — a nutritionally complete meal on their own. Rice supplies the missing amino acids that beans lack and vice-versa; together they are a full protein for a fraction of the cost of meat. In Nicaragua this is not a side dish, it is the spine of the daily table, and gallo pinto is the national breakfast. Learning to make it excellent instead of merely edible is the single best return on effort in this whole manual.

The reason people fear rice is that it fails silently. There is no browning to read, no probe to stick in. You commit water and heat, walk away, and 15 minutes later discover mush, or a crunchy core, or a scorched base — with no idea which knob was wrong. This lesson gives you the instruments: the granule model that explains every outcome, the exact ratios to start from, and the experiments to calibrate them to your stove, pot, and local rice.

Why this framing works for you

Rice is extraction with the variables flipped. In coffee you push hot water through a solid to pull solubles out. In rice you drive water into a solid to swell it, and you are managing the same three levers — dose (water ratio), temperature, and time — plus one you already respect: grind, except here "grind" is the grain's amylose content, fixed by variety. Read the variety, set the ratio, hold the temperature, don't peek. That's the whole loop.

02

The mental model: one granule, two philosophies

Every grain of rice, every bean, every kernel of barley is a package of starch granules — microscopic beads made of two molecules:

  • Amylose — long, straight chains. They pack tightly, hold their shape, and when cooked grains cool they re-associate and firm up. High amylose reads as fluffy, separate, distinct grains. Basmati and other long-grain rices are high-amylose (~22–28%).
  • Amylopectin — huge, bushy, branched molecules. They swell, leak out of the granule, and turn the surrounding water into glue. High amylopectin reads as sticky, creamy, cohesive. Short-grain sushi rice, glutinous ("sweet") rice, and risotto rice are amylopectin-dominant; glutinous rice is nearly 0% amylose.

Heat a granule in water past its gelatinisation temperature — roughly 60–75 °C / 140–167 °F depending on variety — and it drinks water, swells to several times its size, and its ordered crystalline structure melts into a soft gel. That single event is "cooked." Everything else — fluffy vs creamy, separate vs sticky — is decided by the amylose/amylopectin ratio and by how much you agitate the granule while it is soft and leaking.

The variable that decides everything

Two levers: amylose (fixed by variety) and agitation (your choice). Fluffy rice and risotto use identical physics and opposite tactics. Fluffy = strip the surface starch and never disturb the grains. Creamy = keep the surface starch and deliberately scrub it off into the liquid. Fried rice and gallo pinto are a third case: cook fluffy first, dry it out, then fry so the grains stay separate under high heat instead of clumping.

Why the ratio isn't one number — and where "1:2" comes from

If you were taught 1:2, you weren't taught wrong — you were taught a different setup. The trick is to split the water into two jobs: a grain absorbs a roughly fixed amount (about its own weight) to gelatinise; everything above that just evaporates. So the ratio you need is absorption (fixed per grain) + evaporation (set by your pot, not the rice). Three consequences fall out of that:

① A tight-lidded, heavy pot loses little water — lean to the low end (~1:1.5 for rinsed long-grain); a loose lid or wide pan evaporates more, so you add more. ② Evaporation scales with surface area, not volume, so big batches need proportionally less water than small ones — which is exactly what the finger-knuckle method measures that a fixed ratio can't. ③ The familiar 1:2 is a forgiving, evaporation-buffered default (standard for unrinsed, medium-grain, or converted white) and is genuinely correct for brown rice, whose bran demands the extra water and time. These figures assume rinsed grain in a covered pot — run experiment E1 below to lock in your number.

03

Key concepts

Gelatinisation

What: starch granules swell and turn to gel above ~60–75 °C / 140–167 °F in water. Why it matters: this is "cooked" — below it, chalky centre; way past it with excess water, mush. Mistake: cranking heat to rush it, which boils the outside dry before the core reaches temperature.

Amylose vs amylopectin

What: the two starch molecules; their ratio is fixed by variety. Why it matters: it sets whether a grain can be fluffy or sticky. Example: basmati (high amylose) never goes creamy; arborio (high amylopectin) is built for it. Mistake: buying the wrong variety and blaming your technique.

Rinsing = surface-starch removal

What: free amylopectin dust clings to milled grains; rinsing washes it off. Why it matters: that dust is the glue that clumps fluffy rice. Example: rinse basmati until water runs clear → separate grains. Mistake: rinsing risotto rice — you'd wash away the creaminess you want.

Retrogradation (day-old rice)

What: as cooked rice cools, amylose re-crystallises and the grains firm and dry. Why it matters: firm, dry grains fry instead of steaming to mush. Example: fried rice and gallo pinto want cold, day-old rice. Mistake: frying fresh hot rice → gummy paste.

Absorption vs pasta method

What: cook in exactly the water the grain will drink (absorption), or in excess and drain (pasta). Why it matters: absorption keeps starch and nutrients in; pasta method guarantees no gummy pool but dilutes flavour. Mistake: using absorption ratios but lifting the lid, so steam escapes and the count is wrong.

Pectin in beans

What: bean cell walls are cemented by pectin; cooking softens it while internal starch gelatinises. Why it matters: it explains why acid (which stiffens pectin) keeps beans hard and must go in late. Mistake: the salt myth — salt does NOT harden beans; acid, hard water, and old age do.

04

Tools & equipment

Rice and beans are gloriously low-tech. The single highest-value upgrade is a heavy pot with a tight lid — it controls the one thing that ruins rice, escaping steam. Everything else is optional.

Buying guidance
NecessaryA heavy-based pot (2–4 L) with a well-fitting lid. Thick base = even heat, no scorch spot. A cheap cast-aluminium caldero is ideal and standard in Nicaraguan kitchens.
NecessaryFine-mesh sieve for rinsing rice and quinoa, and draining beans. ~US$5.
Worth itRice cooker — offloads the "hold a low simmer and don't peek" job entirely. If you eat rice most days, this pays for itself in reliability. A carbon-steel wok for fried rice: high, dry heat = real "wok hei".
Worth itPressure cooker / olla de presión for dried beans: 25–40 min vs 1–2 h, and cheaper than buying canned forever. The best bean tool there is.
OptionalDigital scale — ratios by weight are more repeatable than by volume, but volume works fine for rice once calibrated.
Waste of money"Fuzzy-logic" luxury rice cookers for a beginner, single-purpose bean pots, and anything marketed as a "rice rinser." Your hand and a sieve are free.
Mediocre → Excellent

The tell of a good cook here isn't gear, it's the lid stays shut. Every peek dumps the steam that's doing the cooking and resets the timing. Learn to read doneness by the sound (a faint crackle when the free water is gone) and by resting off-heat, not by looking.

05

Technique breakdown

L1 Functional

Perfect fluffy white rice — absorption method

  1. Prep — rinse. Put rice in a sieve, rinse under cold water 20–30 s, swirling, until the runoff goes from milky to nearly clear. This strips the surface amylopectin that causes clumping. (Skip this for sticky/sushi rice.)
  2. Prep — measure. Long-grain: 1 part rice : 1.5 parts water by volume as your starting ratio. Basmati closer to 1.5; jasmine closer to 1.25. Salt the water (~1% — about ¾ tsp per cup of rice); it seasons through and does no harm.
  3. Execute — bring up, then choke it down. Bring to a boil uncovered, stir once, then drop to the lowest simmer, cover, and leave it. High heat here only scorches the base while the top starves for water. Target ~12–15 min for white long-grain.
  4. Execute — don't peek. The lid holds the steam that cooks the top layer. Lifting it breaks the count. Trust the clock and the quiet crackle at the end.
  5. Evaluate — rest covered. Off the heat, lid on, 10 min. This lets residual water redistribute from wet base to drier top and lets grains firm up. This step is non-negotiable and fixes 90% of "wet on the bottom" complaints.
  6. Correction — fluff, then read. Fork through gently. Crunchy centre → next time +2 tbsp water or +2 min. Wet/claggy → −2 tbsp water, or rest longer with a tea-towel under the lid to catch condensation. Log it (E1).
L2 Consistent

Pilaf method — toast, then absorb

The upgrade that makes rice taste like a dish rather than a filler. You coat each grain in fat before adding liquid, which waterproofs the surface so grains stay separate and adds a toasted, nutty flavour.

  1. Prep. Rinse and drain rice well. Dice aromatics (onion, garlic) fine.
  2. Execute — sweat & toast. Soften aromatics in 1–2 tbsp fat, add the drained rice, and stir 2–3 min until the grains smell toasty and turn slightly translucent at the edges. The fat coat is what keeps them distinct.
  3. Execute — deglaze & absorb. Add hot stock (same 1:1.5 ratio), bring to a boil, cover, drop to low, 12–15 min. Hot liquid avoids a temperature crash that makes cooking uneven.
  4. Evaluate & correct. Rest 10 min, fluff. Grains should be separate and glossy. Gummy → you skipped or rushed the toast, or didn't drain the rinse water.
L3 Optimisation

Risotto — the anti-fluffy technique

Everything you just learned, inverted. You want the surface starch, so you don't rinse; you want to abrade the grain, so you stir constantly; and you add hot stock a ladle at a time so the grains scrub against each other and release amylopectin into a creamy emulsion of starch, fat, and stock.

  1. Prep. Keep stock hot on a back burner (~1:3–4 total by volume, added gradually). Do not rinse the arborio/carnaroli. Have wine, onion, butter, and cheese ready.
  2. Execute — toast & deglaze. Sweat onion in butter, add rice, toast 2 min, add a splash of wine and let it cook off. Season lightly now.
  3. Execute — the stir. Add hot stock one ladle at a time, stirring steadily, adding the next only when the last is nearly absorbed. 18–22 min. The friction is the technique — it's what pulls starch out to thicken the liquid.
  4. Evaluate — al dente check. A grain should be creamy outside, with a faint firm core. It should spread on the plate ("all'onda" — like a wave), not stand in a mound.
  5. Correction — mantecatura. Off the heat, beat in cold butter and grated hard cheese vigorously. Off-heat is critical — it emulsifies the fat into a glossy sauce instead of letting it split. Loosen with a splash of stock if stiff.
L2 Consistent

Dried beans from scratch

  1. Prep — sort & (optionally) soak. Pick out stones and shrivelled beans. Soaking 8–12 h (or a quick boil-then-rest 1 h) speeds cooking and makes it more even, but is not strictly required — unsoaked beans just take longer. Discarding soak water reduces the oligosaccharides that cause gas.
  2. Execute — gentle simmer, salted. Cover with 5 cm water, add salt (~1 tsp/cup dried) and aromatics. Salt from the start is fine and improves texture — it does not keep beans hard. Keep a bare simmer; a hard boil bursts skins.
  3. Execute — hold acid back. Add tomato, vinegar, or citrus only in the last 20–30 min. Acid stiffens the pectin and can stall softening for an hour. Same with very hard tap water — a pinch of baking soda counters it if your beans stubbornly refuse to soften.
  4. Evaluate — doneness. A done bean is creamy to the very centre with no chalky core and crushes to paste against the roof of your mouth. Times: 1–2 h simmered, 25–40 min in a pressure cooker. Old beans may never fully soften — buy from a shop with turnover.
  5. Correction — keep the liquid. The cloudy cooking broth (caldo) is pure flavour and starch. Reduce it to thicken the pot, or reserve it to season rice and gallo pinto. Never pour it down the drain.
06

Beginner mistakes & troubleshooting

Rice is mushy / claggy Fault
Causes
Too much water; didn't rinse (surface starch glued it); over-stirred; simmered too long; skipped the rest so the base stayed wet.
Diagnose
If the whole pot is uniformly wet → too much water. If only the surface is gluey but grains are cooked → didn't rinse. If it firmed up after resting → you just judged it too early.
Fix
Cut water by 2–3 tbsp per cup next time; rinse until nearly clear; leave the lid on and don't stir mid-cook; rest 10 min off-heat before judging. To rescue a wet batch now: spread on a tray, low oven 5 min to dry.
Crunchy / chalky centre Fault
Causes
Not enough water; heat too high so the outside boiled dry before the core gelatinised; lid leaked steam; you peeked and lost it.
Diagnose
Grains firm but the pot is dry → ran out of water early (too little, or heat too high). Water still pooling but centres hard → uneven heat / poor lid.
Fix
Add 2–3 tbsp hot water, cover, and give it 3–5 min more on the lowest heat, then rest. Next time raise the ratio slightly and simmer lower.
Scorched / burnt bottom Fault
Causes
Heat too high; thin pot with a hot spot; not enough water for the pot's evaporation rate.
Diagnose
Smell of toast + a dark disc on the base. In Nicaragua this is raspa/pega — sometimes prized, but not when unintended.
Fix
Don't stir the burnt layer up — tip the good rice out from the top and leave the scorched disc behind. Next time: lowest possible simmer, heavier pot, or a heat diffuser under a thin one.
Fried rice / gallo pinto turned to mush Fault
Causes
Used fresh, hot, moist rice — it steamed and clumped instead of frying; pan not hot enough; overcrowded so it stewed in its own steam.
Diagnose
Grains stuck in gummy clumps rather than bouncing separate in the pan.
Fix
Use cold, day-old rice (or spread fresh rice on a tray and chill/dry it 30–60 min). Cook in a screaming-hot wide pan, in batches, and don't stir constantly — let it sit to sear, then toss.
Beans that never soften Fault
Causes
Old beans (the #1 cause — age crosslinks the cell walls permanently); acid added too early; very hard (calcium/magnesium-rich) water. Not salt — that myth is wrong.
Diagnose
If a whole bag resists after 2+ h with no acid added → the beans are old; buy fresher. If they stalled right after you added tomato/lime → acid.
Fix
Add a pinch (~⅛ tsp/cup) of baking soda to raise pH and soften pectin; hold all acid until the last 20 min. For genuinely old beans, no fix — replace them.
Beans taste flat / watery Fault
Causes
No aromatic base; under-salted; too much water diluting the broth; threw away the cooking liquid.
Diagnose
They taste "boiled" rather than savoury — no depth, no back-note.
Fix
Build a sofrito (onion, garlic, sweet pepper, a little fat) and either cook the beans on it or stir it in; salt in layers and taste; reduce the broth to concentrate it; finish with a splash of acid at the very end to lift it.
07

Controlled experiments

Change one variable, hold the rest, taste the difference. This is how you calibrate the generic ratios to your kitchen and feel the mechanisms instead of memorising them.

Controlled experiment · E1 · Rinsed vs unrinsed
Goal
Feel what surface starch does to fluffy rice.
Keep constant
Same long-grain rice, same 1:1.5 ratio, same pot, same heat and time, same rest.
Change
Pot A: rinsed until clear. Pot B: unrinsed, straight from the bag.
Expected
A = separate, distinct grains. B = noticeably stickier, clumpier, gluier surface.
Observe
Colour of the rinse water; how the grains fall off a fork. This is your calibration run for your stove's ideal water number too — adjust ±2 tbsp and re-run.
Controlled experiment · E2 · Fresh vs day-old rice for frying
Goal
See why retrogradation matters for fried rice and gallo pinto.
Keep constant
Same rice, same pan and heat, same oil, same tossing.
Change
Batch A: fresh, hot, just-cooked rice. Batch B: cooked yesterday, cold from the fridge.
Expected
A steams, clumps, and goes gummy. B fries up separate, with light browning and a firmer bite.
Observe
The sound in the pan (steam hiss vs dry sizzle) and whether grains bounce free or stick together.
Controlled experiment · E3 · Salt from start vs at the end (bean myth)
Goal
Kill the "salt keeps beans hard" myth with your own tongue.
Keep constant
Same beans (same bag/age), same water, same pot, same simmer and time. Same total salt.
Change
Pot A: salted at the start. Pot B: salted only at the end.
Expected
A is at least as tender as B — usually more evenly seasoned and slightly better in texture (the skins hold together). A stays hard? — not the salt.
Observe
Softness of the centre and how far the seasoning has penetrated (bite one from each rinsed of broth).
Controlled experiment · E4 · Acid early vs late
Goal
Isolate the real culprit — acid — behind hard beans.
Keep constant
Same fresh beans, water, salt, heat, time.
Change
Pot A: add 2 tbsp tomato paste / a splash of vinegar at the start. Pot B: add the same acid in the last 20 min.
Expected
A is measurably firmer/chalkier at the same cook time; B is fully tender. This is pectin stiffening at low pH.
Observe
Timing to reach creamy-centre doneness in each pot — A can lag by 30–60 min.

Practice system

L1 — plain rice never fails L2 — beans and pilaf on demand L3 — tune texture at will L4 — improvise a meal from the pantry
Practice drill · Beginner

The 5-batch rice calibration

Goal
Find the exact water ratio and time for your rice + pot + stove.
Difficulty
Beginner
Materials
One bag of long-grain rice, your usual pot, a timer, a notebook.
Steps
Cook the same 1 cup of rice five times, changing only the water: 1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 1.75, 2.0. Rinse and rest identically each time. Log the result.
Success
You can name your ratio ("1 : 1.6, 13 min, rest 10") and hit fluffy rice every time.
Failure modes
Changing two things at once (water and heat); skipping the rest; peeking.
Improve
Repeat with basmati and jasmine — note how the ideal number shifts down with lower amylose.
Practice drill · Intermediate

Weekly bean pot

Goal
Make a big pot of seasoned red beans you can eat all week and turn into gallo pinto.
Difficulty
Intermediate
Materials
500 g dried red beans, onion, garlic, sweet pepper, salt, a little oil.
Steps
Build a sofrito, add sorted beans + water + salt, simmer to creamy centre (or pressure-cook 30 min), hold any acid to the end. Reserve the broth.
Success
Creamy, well-seasoned beans with rich broth; no chalky centres; keeps 4–5 days refrigerated.
Failure modes
Bland (no sofrito / under-salted); split skins (boiled too hard); tough (old beans or early acid).
Improve
Reduce leftover broth to a glaze; mash a ladle of beans back in for body (refried-style).
Practice drill · Intermediate

Quinoa & couscous — the two 10-minute grains

Goal
Add two fast starches to your rotation and learn why they're handled differently.
Difficulty
Intermediate
Materials
Quinoa, couscous, a sieve, hot stock or water, fat, salt.
Steps
Quinoa: rinse hard (removes bitter saponin coating), optionally toast in a dry pan, then 1:2 with liquid, simmer covered ~15 min, rest 5, fluff. Couscous: it's steamed semolina pasta, not a grain — just pour equal-volume boiling seasoned liquid + a knob of fat over it, cover 5 min, fluff with a fork.
Success
Quinoa fluffy with visible curled germ, no bitterness; couscous light and separate.
Failure modes
Bitter quinoa (didn't rinse); mushy couscous (too much water or boiled it like rice).
Improve
Cook quinoa in bean broth; stir herbs, lemon, and oil through couscous while warm.
09

Projects

Project · Beginner

Gallo pinto — the Nicaraguan staple

Objective
The dish that justifies this whole module: day-old rice and red beans fried together into breakfast for pennies.
Skills trained
Retrogradation (day-old rice), sofrito, using bean broth, high-heat frying without mush.
Method
Fry finely diced onion and sweet pepper in a little oil until soft. Add drained cooked red beans, then cold day-old white rice (roughly 2 parts rice : 1 part beans). Toss over medium-high heat so grains stay separate; loosen with a splash of the reserved bean broth and, if you have it, a dash of Salsa Lizano. Finish with chopped coriander.
Expected result
Distinct, lightly fried grains tinged red-brown from the beans, savoury and glossy — not a wet clump. Serve with egg, cheese, or plantain.
Evaluation
Grains separate and bouncy (day-old rice worked); balanced salt; broth gives sheen without sogginess.
Project · Intermediate

Restaurant-grade egg fried rice

Objective
Chase "wok hei" — the smoky, dry, separate-grained fried rice of a good stall.
Skills trained
High-heat management, not crowding the pan, sequencing add-ins, day-old rice discipline.
Method
Get the wide pan/wok as hot as it goes. Scramble egg, remove. Sear aromatics, add cold day-old rice, press it into the metal, let it sit to toast, then toss. Season (soy, a pinch of sugar, white pepper), fold the egg back, finish with spring onion. Work in one thin layer — batch if needed.
Expected result
Every grain distinct and slightly chewy, faint char aroma, egg in soft curds throughout.
Evaluation
No gummy clumps, no pooled sauce, an audible dry sizzle the whole time.
Project · Advanced

Mushroom risotto — and a barley "orzotto" variant

Objective
Execute the creamy, agitation-driven end of the spectrum, then prove you understand the mechanism by swapping the grain.
Skills trained
Gradual hot-stock addition, constant stirring for controlled amylopectin release, al dente judgement, off-heat mantecatura emulsion.
Method
Standard risotto (see §05, L3) with sautéed mushrooms folded in. Then repeat with pearl barley in place of arborio: barley is a whole grain that needs a longer simmer (~40 min) but releases beta-glucan, so it goes naturally creamy with less babysitting — a cheaper, nuttier "orzotto".
Expected result
Both spread all'onda (like a wave), glossy from the butter-cheese emulsion, with a firm core.
Evaluation
Creamy without being gluey; emulsion stays glossy (added butter/cheese off-heat); barley version creamy from its own beta-glucan, not from added cream.

Assessment

You've got this module when you can explain the outcomes, not just reproduce a recipe. Answer these from the mechanism.

Why can basmati never be made creamy like risotto, no matter how much you stir it — and what property would you change to make it possible?

Fried rice and risotto both involve starch and stirring, yet one wants dry separate grains and the other wants a creamy pool. Explain the opposite goals in terms of amylopectin and agitation.

Your beans have simmered 2 hours and are still chalky. Walk through the three real suspects (in order of likelihood) and how you'd tell them apart. Which common "cause" can you rule out immediately?

You lift the lid on absorbing rice "just to check." Trace the chain of consequences on the final texture.

Practical challenge

Cook one pot of dried red beans and use it two ways in 48 hours: a seasoned bean bowl the first night, and gallo pinto with day-old rice the next morning. Reserve and use the bean broth in both. Success = creamy well-seasoned beans, and pinto with distinct, separate, glossy grains — no mush, nothing wasted.
