MODULE 06 · FERMENTATION

Controlled Rot That Makes Food Better

Fermentation is applied microbiology you can taste — control salt, oxygen, and temperature and beneficial microbes preserve food and generate flavour you cannot buy.

Month 6–7 Start early Lacto + koji
01

Why this matters

You already know how to extract flavour from a coffee bean by controlling variables — dose, water, temperature, time. Fermentation is the same discipline pointed at a jar. You set the boundary conditions (salt concentration, oxygen availability, temperature) and then microbes do the work, converting a raw vegetable into something acidic, complex, and shelf-stable. You are not adding flavour from a bottle; you are cultivating it from the raw material's own sugars and proteins.

The payoff for a home cook is leverage. A jar of kraut costs pennies in cabbage and salt, needs ten minutes of attention, and then improves every plate you make for a month — a sharp acidic counterpoint to rich food, the thing restaurant plates have and home plates usually lack. Kimchi, hot sauce, and pickled onions do the same. And at the far end, koji and miso open a door to umami that no shop-bought ingredient reaches, because the good stuff is measured in months and nobody sells patience.

It is also the cheapest, safest way to learn to trust a process over a recipe. A ferment does not care about your timer. It cares whether the pH has dropped. Once you internalise that, you stop cooking by superstition.

Why it works

Fermentation is preservation by acidification. Bacteria eat sugar and excrete lactic acid, which drops the pH low enough that nothing dangerous can grow. You are deliberately losing a race in favour of the microbe you want, by rigging the starting conditions so only it can thrive.

02

Mental model — the four barriers

Think of a ferment as a selective environment. You are not sterilising anything; wild microbes are everywhere, including the ones you want. Your job is to build a set of barriers that favour beneficial Lactobacillus and its lactic-acid-producing friends while starving or poisoning everything that would spoil the food or make you ill. There are four knobs.

Salt is selection pressure. A 2–3% brine (by weight) is uncomfortable for most spoilage bacteria and moulds but tolerable for salt-hardy lactic bacteria. It gives your team a head start. Oxygen exclusion is the second barrier: lactic bacteria are happy without air, whereas most spoilage moulds and film yeasts need oxygen. Keep the vegetables submerged under the brine and you close that door. Temperature is the rate-and-selectivity knob — warmer ferments faster but sloppier, cooler is slower but cleaner. And acid is the endgame: as the bacteria work, pH falls, and once it crosses below ~4.6 the food is in the safe zone where Clostridium botulinum cannot grow. The first three barriers buy you time; the acid barrier is what ultimately makes the food safe and stable.

One distinction you must nail before anything else, because it confuses almost everyone:

What beginners get wrong

Calling both "pickles." A jar of cucumbers in hot vinegar is a quick pickle and will never fizz, funk, or turn cloudy — if it does, something is wrong. A jar of cucumbers in 3% brine on the counter is a ferment and should go cloudy and bubble. Confusing the two makes you throw out good ferments and eat spoiled quick-pickles.

03

Key concepts

Lacto-fermentation

What: Naturally present Lactobacillus and relatives convert sugars into lactic acid. Why it matters: The falling pH both preserves the food and generates the sour, savoury flavour. Example: Cabbage + 2% salt becomes sauerkraut with nothing added but time. Common mistake: Expecting a starter culture — you almost never need one; the microbes are already on the vegetable.

The salt barrier

What: A 2–3% brine selects for salt-tolerant lactic bacteria and against spoilage organisms. Why it matters: Too little salt and you invite mush and rot; too much and even the good bacteria stall. Example: 3% brine keeps a cucumber crunchy and safe while it sours. Common mistake: Guessing salt by volume — you must weigh it.

Anaerobic environment

What: Oxygen excluded by keeping solids submerged under brine. Why it matters: Lactic bacteria thrive without air; the moulds and film yeasts that spoil ferments need air. Example: A weight that holds cabbage under the liquid keeps kraut clean. Common mistake: "Keep it under the brine" ignored — the exposed bit at the top grows fuzz.

Microbial succession

What: Different microbes dominate in sequence as conditions change. Why it matters: The early gas-and-aroma phase (Leuconostoc) then the acidifying phase (Lactobacillus) create the flavour arc. Example: Young kimchi tastes bright and fizzy; aged kimchi tastes deep and sour. Common mistake: Judging a ferment on day 2 and binning it before it has developed.

The pH 4.6 line

What: The safety threshold below which C. botulinum cannot grow or produce toxin. Why it matters: This single number is why a submerged ferment is safe at room temperature. Example: A finished kraut sits near pH 3.4 — far under the line. Common mistake: Assuming "fermented = safe" before the acid has actually developed; the first day or two is the vulnerable window, which the salt barrier covers.

Koji & enzymatic ferments

What: Mould-driven ferments (miso, tempeh) where fungal enzymes, not just acid, transform the substrate. Why it matters: Aspergillus oryzae breaks proteins into amino acids — pure umami. Example: Soybeans + salt + koji become miso over 6–12 months. Common mistake: Treating these like quick lacto-ferments; they are long-clock projects on a different mechanism.

04

Tools & equipment

Fermentation is deliberately low-tech — the microbes bring the technology. The one instrument that changes everything is a scale, because salt percentage is the master variable and you cannot eyeball a percentage.

Kit — necessary / optional / waste of money
Digital scale (0.1 g)Necessary. Weighs salt and water/vegetable so you hit an exact %.
Wide-mouth glass jarsNecessary. Non-reactive, lets you see the brine line. 500 ml–1 L.
A weight to submerge solidsNecessary. Glass fermentation weight, a small water-filled zip bag, or a boiled stone. Keeps food under brine.
Non-iodised saltNecessary-ish. Fine sea salt or pickling salt. Iodine and anti-caking agents can inhibit bacteria / cloud brine. Kosher works if you weigh it.
Airlock lids / burpingOptional. An airlock vents CO₂ while blocking air. Or just "burp" a loosely-lidded jar daily. Both work.
pH strips or meterOptional but engineer-satisfying. Confirms you crossed 4.6. A meter (~0.1 resolution) beats strips for reading acidic samples.
Fancy ceramic crockWaste of money to start. Beautiful, expensive, and does nothing a jar and a weight don't. Buy one after your tenth batch, not your first.
"Probiotic starter" powdersMostly waste of money for vegetables. The culture is already on the produce. Starters matter for kombucha (SCOBY), tempeh (spores), koji.
Variable that matters

Water. Heavily chlorinated tap water can inhibit your bacteria. If your tap smells of chlorine, leave it out overnight (chlorine off-gasses) or use filtered water. Chloramine does not off-gas — filter it. This is the single silent cause of "my ferment did nothing."

05

Technique breakdown — a lacto-ferment, generalised

L1 Functional

Every lacto-vegetable ferment is the same four moves. Learn them once and kraut, kimchi, dill pickles, and fermented hot sauce are all variations.

  1. Prep — hit your salt target by weight. Two methods. Brine method (loose or hard vegetables like cucumbers, beans, whole chillies): make a 2–3% brine and pour it over. Dry-salt method (shreddable, watery vegetables like cabbage): weigh the vegetable, add 2% of that weight in salt, and massage until it releases its own brine. Reasoning: both routes land the vegetable in roughly 2–3% salt water — the dry-salt route just borrows the water from inside the cabbage.
  2. Execute — submerge and exclude air. Pack solids into the jar and press until liquid rises above them. Add a weight so nothing floats into the air gap. Lid on loosely, or use an airlock. Reasoning: the food under the brine is anaerobic and protected; anything poking into the air will grow film or mould.
  3. Evaluate — read the process, not the clock. Day 1–3: bubbles, cloudiness, a fresh sour smell — all correct. Taste from day 4 or 5 onward. It is "done" when it is as sour as you like it. Reasoning: temperature makes "done" land anywhere from 5 days (warm) to 4 weeks (cool). The tongue and, if you like, a pH strip below 4.6 are your real gauges.
  4. Correct — move it to the fridge to lock the state. When you like the flavour, cap it tightly and refrigerate. Cold slows the bacteria to a crawl so it holds roughly where you stopped it. Reasoning: fermentation never truly stops, it just slows; refrigeration is your "pause" button, keeping a batch pleasant for months instead of marching on to vinegar.
Mediocre → Excellent

A mediocre fermenter follows a recipe's day count and bottles on schedule. An excellent one tastes every two days, notes the room temperature, and learns the personal map of "at 22 °C my kraut peaks at day 12." After three batches you stop needing the recipe because you own the curve.

06

Beginner mistakes & troubleshooting

L2 Consistent
White film on the surface — is it kahm yeast or mould? Common
Causes
A flat, white, sometimes wrinkled skin on the brine is kahm yeast — a harmless surface yeast that grows when there's air contact and the ferment is on the warm/slow side. Fuzzy, raised, and coloured (green, black, blue, pink) growth is mould — not harmless.
Diagnose
Kahm is flat and dull-white, smells yeasty, and skims off. Mould is three-dimensional and fuzzy like bread mould, in colours. Anything on the submerged vegetables is essentially never mould; problems live at the air surface.
Fix
Kahm: skim it off, make sure everything's back under the brine, keep going — the ferment underneath is fine. Mould: discard the batch. Do not "scrape and continue" with fuzzy coloured mould; its threads penetrate below the surface. Prevent both by keeping solids submerged and the room cooler.
Pickles came out soft or slimy Texture
Causes
Too little salt (spoilage/pectin-degrading microbes win), too warm a ferment, old/soft cucumbers to begin with, or blossom ends left on (they carry pectinase enzymes that soften).
Diagnose
Slimy brine with an off, rotten smell = failed, salt too low or contamination — bin it. Merely soft but pleasant-smelling and sour = safe but sad, usually heat or weak salt.
Fix
Go to 3–3.5% brine for cucumbers, trim ~2 mm off the blossom end, add a tannin source (a grape/oak/bay/black-tea leaf) for crunch, and ferment somewhere cooler. Use firm, fresh cucumbers.
Too sour, or not sour enough Flavour
Causes
Too sour = fermented too long or too warm before refrigerating. Not sour = too cold, too much salt stalling the bacteria, chlorinated water, or simply not enough time.
Diagnose
No bubbles and no souring after 4–5 days at room temperature points to inhibition (salt too high or chlorine). Rapidly turning mouth-puckering points to heat.
Fix
Too sour: refrigerate earlier next time; use the over-sour batch as you would vinegar. Not sour: verify salt is ~2%, switch to filtered/off-gassed water, and give it warmth and time.
Sauerkraut turned mushy Texture
Causes
Salt too low, ferment too warm, or shredded too fine. Mush is pectin breakdown accelerated by heat and weak salt.
Diagnose
If it smells clean and sour but is limp, it's texture, not safety. Off/putrid smell means it failed — discard.
Fix
Hold 2% salt precisely, shred a touch coarser, and ferment at 16–20 °C / 61–68 °F rather than a warm kitchen.
Kombucha won't fizz / second ferment is flat Kombucha
Causes
Not enough residual/added sugar for the second ferment, bottles not sealed airtight, or too cold. Fizz is CO₂ from yeast eating sugar in a sealed bottle.
Diagnose
No sugar left + loose cap = no carbonation. A living, ropey SCOBY and active first ferment but flat bottles points at the seal or the sugar.
Fix
Add a measured teaspoon of sugar (or a little fruit juice) per bottle, seal tight, leave 2–4 days at room temp, then refrigerate. Do not over-sugar or leave sealed too long at room temp — bottle bombs are a real hazard. Burp the bottles to check pressure; use pressure-rated bottles.
When do I just throw it out? Safety
Causes
Genuine failures do happen — usually from weak salt, warmth, and air exposure combined.
Diagnose
Discard if you see fuzzy coloured mould, if it smells putrid/rotten (not sour-funky but genuinely bad — you'll know), if the texture is slimy with an off smell, or if it never soured at all after a week (no acid barrier formed). Trust the combination of smell, appearance, and whether the process actually happened.
Fix
When in doubt, throw it out — a jar of cabbage is cheap. But note: cloudiness, bubbles, a strong funky-sour smell, and sediment are all normal and not reasons to discard.
What beginners get wrong

Panicking at normal signs. Cloudy brine, vigorous bubbling, a pungent sour smell, and white sediment at the bottom are what success looks like — they are not spoilage. The two things that actually signal danger are fuzzy coloured mould and a genuinely putrid (not sour) smell. Learn to tell "funky" from "foul."

07

Controlled experiments

L3 Optimization

Change one variable, hold the rest, taste side by side. This is how you build your personal map instead of memorising someone else's recipe.

Controlled experiment · E1 — salt concentration
Goal
Feel what salt % does to texture, speed, and flavour.
Keep constant
Same cucumbers (cut from the same batch), same jar size, same spot in the kitchen, same day started, same dill/garlic.
Change
Jar A: 2% brine. Jar B: 3.5% brine.
Expected
Jar A ferments faster and tastes brighter but risks softening; Jar B is slower, firmer, saltier, cleaner.
Observe
Which crunches more at day 7? Which soured first? Which would you make again? Record the room temperature.
Controlled experiment · E2 — temperature
Goal
See temperature as the rate-and-cleanliness knob.
Keep constant
Same shredded cabbage, same 2% salt, same jar type, started same hour.
Change
Jar A: warm spot (~24 °C / 75 °F). Jar B: cool spot (~16 °C / 61 °F).
Expected
Warm jar bubbles hard and sours in days but risks off-flavours and softness; cool jar takes weeks and tastes cleaner and crunchier.
Observe
Days to your preferred sourness in each. Any kahm yeast (favours warmth)? Which tastes "cleaner"?
Controlled experiment · E3 — submerged vs exposed
Goal
Prove to yourself why "keep it under the brine" is the rule, not a suggestion.
Keep constant
Same kraut mix, salt, jar, and spot.
Change
Jar A: weighted so all cabbage stays submerged. Jar B: no weight, some cabbage left poking above the brine.
Expected
Jar A stays clean; Jar B grows kahm yeast or mould on the exposed shreds while the submerged part is fine.
Observe
Exactly where growth appears (the air line). This is the single most convincing demo of the anaerobic barrier. Safety note: if Jar B grows fuzzy coloured mould, discard the whole jar — do not eat the experiment.
Controlled experiment · E4 (optional) — succession over time
Goal
Taste the flavour arc of microbial succession.
Keep constant
One large batch of kimchi, one jar, one temperature.
Change
Only when you taste: day 2, day 5, day 10, day 21.
Expected
Bright and fizzy early (Leuconostoc), deep and sour later (Lactobacillus dominant).
Observe
Which stage you prefer — that tells you when to refrigerate future batches.
08

Practice system — repeatable drills

L2 Consistent
Practice drill

Weigh a 3% brine, blind

Goal
Make hitting a salt percentage automatic.
Difficulty
Beginner
Materials
Scale, water, salt.
Steps
For 3% brine: weigh water, add 3% of that weight in salt (1 L water = 1000 g → 30 g salt). Do 2%, 2.5%, 3% until you can compute grams in your head.
Success
You can state "700 ml water needs 21 g salt for 3%" without a calculator.
Failure modes
Measuring salt by spoon; forgetting brands differ in crystal size, so a spoonful varies by 2×.
Improve
Always weigh, never scoop. Tape a brine table inside a cupboard.
Practice drill

The daily taste-and-log

Goal
Learn to read a ferment by process, not clock.
Difficulty
Beginner
Materials
Any active ferment, a notebook.
Steps
Each day, log room temp, bubbling, smell, and a one-word flavour note. Refrigerate the day it hits your ideal.
Success
You can predict "done" within a day for your kitchen.
Failure modes
Not tasting, then finding it over-soured; forgetting to note temperature (the hidden driver).
Improve
Add a pH strip reading to correlate taste with acidity crossing 4.6.
Practice drill

Kahm-vs-mould identification

Goal
Kill the fear that makes you bin good batches.
Difficulty
Beginner
Materials
Photos (see §10) plus your own jars.
Steps
Whenever film appears, classify it before acting: flat dull-white (kahm, skim and continue) vs fuzzy coloured (mould, discard).
Success
You act correctly without a second guess.
Failure modes
Reflexively discarding kahm; reflexively scraping mould and continuing.
Improve
Prevent both by keeping solids submerged and the room cool.
09

Projects — beginner to advanced

L1 Functional
Project · Beginner

Quick-pickled red onions (the gateway — and a quick pickle, not a ferment)

Objective
Get an instant win and cement the ferment-vs-quick-pickle distinction. Thin-slice a red onion, pack in a jar, cover with warm 1:1 vinegar-to-water plus a spoon of salt and sugar. Ready in an hour, pink and sharp.
Skills trained
Knife work, the acid-added model, restraint.
Expected result
Bright, crunchy onions that upgrade tacos, salads, and sandwiches immediately.
Evaluation
Crunchy, not mushy; sharp but balanced. Note it never bubbles — because no microbes are working.
Project · Beginner

Sauerkraut — your first true lacto-ferment

Objective
Shred cabbage, weigh it, add 2% salt, massage until it makes its own brine, pack tight under a weight, ferment 1–4 weeks tasting along the way.
Skills trained
Dry-salt method, submersion, reading the process, patience.
Expected result
Crunchy, tangy, faintly funky kraut at ~pH 3.4.
Evaluation
Clean sour smell, still has crunch, no fuzzy mould. This one recipe teaches the whole module.
Project · Beginner

Garlic honey (safe-ish, with rules)

Objective
Submerge peeled garlic cloves in raw honey; over weeks it slowly ferments, thins, and the garlic mellows into something spreadable and mellow-hot.
Skills trained
Understanding water activity as a barrier; burping.
Expected result
Runnier, tangy honey and soft, mild garlic — a cheat-code condiment.
Evaluation
Should bubble gently over time (that's fine — burp the jar). Safety: honey's very low water activity plus acidity suppress C. botulinum, which is why this differs from the genuinely dangerous garlic-in-oil (low-acid, anaerobic, room-temp — a real botulism risk you should never store at room temperature). Keep garlic fully submerged in honey, burp it, refrigerate if unsure, and follow tested guidance for anything you plan to can.
L3 Optimization
Project · Intermediate

Kimchi or fermented hot sauce (pick one)

Objective
Kimchi: salt napa cabbage in ~3% brine, rinse, coat in a paste of gochugaru, garlic, ginger, and fish sauce, pack under its own liquid, ferment 3–7 days then refrigerate. Hot sauce: ferment chillies + garlic in 3% brine for 1–2 weeks, then blend with a splash of the brine and a little vinegar for shelf stability.
Skills trained
Layered flavour building, succession (taste the arc), CO₂ management.
Expected result
A living condiment that transforms rice, eggs, noodles, and everything else.
Evaluation
Balanced heat and acid, effervescence, no off smells. For hot sauce, check it's comfortably acidic (below 4.6) before treating it as shelf-stable; otherwise refrigerate.
Project · Intermediate

Kombucha

Objective
Brew sweet tea, cool it, add a SCOBY and starter liquid, first-ferment 7–10 days until pleasantly tart, then bottle for a 2–4 day second ferment to carbonate.
Skills trained
Managing a symbiotic yeast + acetic-acid-bacteria culture; carbonation control.
Expected result
Tart, lightly fizzy tea; a growing SCOBY.
Evaluation
Acidity keeps it safe; taste for tart-not-cloying. Safety: use glass, not reactive metal, for fermenting; do not over-sugar the sealed second ferment or leave it warm too long — bottle bombs. Burp bottles to gauge pressure.
L4 Mastery
Project · Advanced — the 6-month clock

Your first miso

Objective
Combine cooked, mashed soybeans with rice koji (Aspergillus oryzae) and salt (~6–13% of total weight depending on style/aging), pack tight into a crock under a weight and a salt-cap, and age 6–12 months. Koji enzymes break proteins into amino acids — the source of deep umami. Start this early in the module so it's ready by the end.
Skills trained
Enzymatic (not just acid) fermentation, long-clock patience, salt-as-preservative at scale.
Expected result
A dense, savoury, salty paste you can build soups and marinades around for a year.
Evaluation
Deepening colour and umami over months; surface can be managed. Also on this shelf: tempeh (cooked soybeans bound by Rhizopus into a firm cake over ~24–48 h at a warm, watched ~30 °C / 86 °F — a short, temperature-critical ferment) and cheese (milk acidified and/or renneted, curds worked and aged with controlled cultures and moulds). Frame all three as culture-first, patience-second.

Knowledge check — understanding, not recall

Answer these in your own words before moving on: (1) Why is a submerged 2.5% brine at room temperature safe when leaving those same vegetables on the counter is not? (2) A friend's ferment "did nothing" — no bubbles, no souring after a week. Give two mechanistic causes and how you'd test each. (3) Why is garlic honey reasonably safe but garlic in oil a genuine botulism risk, in terms of the barriers? (4) You want a crunchier, cleaner-tasting kraut. Which one variable do you change, in which direction, and why?

Model answer to (1): The salt selects for lactic bacteria and against spoilage organisms, submersion excludes the oxygen that moulds and film yeasts need, and within days the bacteria drop the pH below 4.6 — the threshold below which C. botulinum and other pathogens cannot grow. Bare vegetables on the counter have none of these barriers: neutral pH, full oxygen, no selection, so spoilage organisms win.

Practical challenge

Run experiments E1–E3 simultaneously (five jars) starting the same afternoon, keep a daily log of temperature, appearance, smell, and flavour, and after two weeks present a side-by-side tasting with a one-paragraph conclusion on how salt, temperature, and submersion each changed the result. Separately, start a miso today so the clock is running.

Reflection

Where did you feel the pull to "check the recipe" instead of tasting the jar? Fermentation rewards reading the process over obeying a timer. Note the moment you first trusted your senses over the day count — that shift, more than any single batch, is the skill this module is really teaching.
