MODULE 01 · PROTEINS

Cook Protein Without Wrecking It

Dry chicken is not a cooking failure — it is a temperature-control failure. Learn to read protein like a thermometer reads a curve: control the target, respect carryover, manage moisture, and the same model that saves your breast meat will hand you a French omelette and a hollandaise.

Month 1 chicken + eggs The dry-chicken fix
01

Why this matters

You keep drying out chicken breast. Almost everyone does, and almost everyone blames the wrong variable — the recipe, the pan, the oil, "bad meat." None of those are the cause. The cause is that you are cooking to a time ("20 minutes a side") when meat only responds to temperature. A breast does not know how long it has been in the pan. It knows how hot its centre is, and past a narrow threshold it violently expels its own water.

Think of it exactly like coffee extraction. You did not learn espresso by timing the shot blindly; you learned it by controlling the variables that drive the outcome — dose, temperature, pressure, grind — and reading the result. Protein is the same instrument. There are four control variables: internal temperature, moisture (salt and surface water), carryover (the heat still moving after you stop cooking), and the cut's own chemistry (how much fat and collagen it carries). Master those four and "cook chicken" stops being a gamble.

The payoff compounds. Eggs are the same problem at a lower, faster, more punishing temperature scale — the most sensitive protein thermometer in your kitchen. So the model you build on a chicken breast this month is the model that later gives you a creamy omelette, an unbroken hollandaise, and eggs that set softly in shakshuka instead of curdling into rubber. One mental model, a whole module of dishes.

Why it works

Muscle is a bundle of protein fibres holding water like a wrung-out sponge held open. Heat makes those proteins denature — unfold — and then contract. As the fibres tighten and the surrounding collagen shrinks, they squeeze the water out. Below a threshold this is gentle. Above it, it is a stampede. Your entire job is to stop before the stampede, or (for thighs) to push past it into a different mechanism entirely.

02

The mental model: a protein is a temperature curve

Here is the single most important picture in this module. Plot moisture retained in chicken breast against internal temperature. It is not a gentle slope — it is a cliff. The meat stays juicy across a wide, forgiving range and then, over just a few degrees around 65 °C / 149 °F, it falls off the edge.

Now the twist that unlocks the whole module: a thigh has a completely different curve. A breast is lean muscle — its curve is a cliff. A thigh is worked muscle, loaded with fat and collagen. Collagen is tough connective tissue that only starts melting into soft, juicy gelatin above ~70 °C / 158 °F and does its best work around 74–88 °C / 165–190 °F. So the very temperature that destroys a breast is the temperature that redeems a thigh. That is why thighs are forgiving and breasts are not — not luck, chemistry.

PropertyBreastThigh
Muscle typeFast-twitch, barely workedSlow-twitch, constantly worked
FatVery leanMarbled + fat cap
CollagenLow — nothing to meltHigh — needs melting to gelatin
Target temp63–65 °C · 145–149 °F74–88 °C · 165–190 °F
Usable window~4 °C (a cliff)~15 °C (a plateau)
ForgivenessLow — punishes overshootHigh — overshoot improves it
Best methodGentle + pull early + rest; brine firstSear hard, render fat, cook through
The variable that matters most

Carryover. When you take meat off heat, the hot outer layers keep dumping energy inward — the centre keeps climbing for several minutes. A thick breast can rise 3–6 °C / 5–11 °F after it leaves the pan. That is why you pull at 60–62 °C and let it coast to 63–65 °C. If you wait until the probe reads your target, you have already overshot. Pull early. Always.

03

Key concepts

Denaturation & contraction

What: heat unfolds muscle proteins, then shrinks the fibres and their collagen sheaths. Why it matters: the shrink squeezes out water — this is "dryness." Example: a breast held at 74 °C loses far more juice than one pulled at 64 °C. Mistake: treating dryness as "not enough sauce" instead of "too much heat."

Pasteurisation is time × temp

What: "safe" is not one number — it's a dose. Chicken held at 60 °C / 140 °F for ~35 min, or 63 °C / 145 °F for ~9 min, is as safe as an instant hit of 74 °C / 165 °F. Why it matters: it lets you cook cooler and safe. Mistake: believing 74 °C is the only safe target — it's just the fastest and driest.

Collagen → gelatin

What: tough connective tissue converts to silky gelatin above ~70 °C, faster the hotter it gets. Why it matters: it's why thighs, drumsticks, and stock improve with more heat and time. Example: stock simmered 4 h sets to jelly when cold. Mistake: pulling a thigh at 65 °C "to keep it juicy" — you get it rubbery instead.

Carryover cooking

What: residual heat keeps raising the core after cooking stops. Why it matters: it's the difference between 64 °C and 70 °C on the same breast. Example: pull at 60 °C, tent, watch it climb to 64 °C. Mistake: ignoring it and cooking "to target" in the pan.

Brining = water-holding

What: salt partially dissolves/denatures muscle proteins so they hold more water and season throughout. Why it matters: a brined breast keeps ~10% more moisture and forgives overshoot. Example: wet ~5–6% salt brine, or ~1% of meat weight as a dry brine. Mistake: salting only the surface 2 minutes before cooking — no time to work.

Resting = redistribution

What: as fibres cool and relax slightly, expelled juice is reabsorbed and stops running out on the cut. Why it matters: a rested breast bleeds far less on the board. Example: rest 5–10 min tented. Mistake: slicing straight off the heat and watching the juice pool on the plate instead of staying in the meat.

Mediocre → Excellent

A mediocre cook owns a thermometer and still overcooks, because they probe once, at the end. An excellent cook probes to learn the carryover of their pan and their cut thickness, then pulls by prediction — "this 3 cm breast in this pan climbs 5 °C, so I pull at 59 °C." Same tool, opposite result. The instrument is only as good as the model behind the hand holding it.

04

Tools & equipment

This module has exactly one non-negotiable tool, and it is cheap. Everything else is convenience.

Kit — necessary / optional / skip
Instant-read probeNecessary. The whole module runs on it. A fast (<3 s) thermocouple is ideal; even a £12 unit beats guessing. This is your refractometer.
Heavy panNecessary. Cast iron or thick stainless holds heat through the cold shock of raw meat and builds fond. Thin non-stick can't brown and won't leave fond for a pan sauce.
Kitchen scaleNecessary. Brining is a percentage of weight. "A handful of salt" is not a spec. 1 g resolution.
Non-stick / carbon steelNecessary for eggs. A dedicated, well-kept non-stick or seasoned carbon-steel pan is the difference between a French omelette and a scramble welded to the metal.
Wire rack + trayOptional, high value. For dry-brining uncovered in the fridge (crisp skin) and for resting without steaming the underside soggy.
Fine strainer / chinoisOptional. Clear stock. A muslin-lined sieve does the same job.
Sous-vide circulatorOptional / later. Makes the temperature curve trivial — you literally set the target. Great teacher, not required to learn the principle on a stove.
Meat "tenderisers", injectors, spray oilsSkip. Solutions to problems that temperature control already solves.
What beginners get wrong

Buying a dial thermometer that lags 20 seconds and reads ±3 °C. On a protein with a 4 °C window, a slow, imprecise probe is worse than useless — it gives you false confidence while the centre races past the cliff. Fast and accurate beats expensive and fancy every time.

05

Technique breakdown

L1 — I can cook it through without drying it L2 — I reproduce juicy, seasoned, crisp results L3 — I tune brine, heat, and carryover to the cut L4 — I improvise across proteins and teach the model
L1 Functional

Pan-seared chicken thigh (the forgiving one — start here)

Thighs first, deliberately. They forgive the temperature mistakes you're still making, so you can practise heat and browning without the cliff punishing you.

  1. Prep. Pat the thigh bone-dry with paper towel and salt it (ideally dry-brined hours ahead — see L2). Why: surface water must boil off before browning can start; every drop delays Maillard and steams the meat grey.
  2. Execute. Skin-side (or presentation-side) down into a moderately hot pan with a little oil. Leave it. Don't poke, don't move it for 6–8 min. Why: the crust releases itself when it's browned; moving it early tears the forming crust and interrupts contact.
  3. Execute. Flip once. Cook to 74–80 °C / 165–176 °F at the thickest point, near but not touching bone. Why: the thigh wants that heat — collagen melts to gelatin and it turns silky, not dry.
  4. Evaluate. Probe, don't guess. Juices near the bone should run clear; flesh pulls easily. Skin should be crisp and shatter slightly.
  5. Correct. Pale skin → pan too cool or meat too wet: dry harder, start hotter, and don't crowd the pan (crowding drops the temperature and steams). Rubbery bite → under 70 °C: cook it further, not less.
L2 Consistent

Brined chicken breast (the dry-chicken fix, executed)

  1. Prep — brine. Dry-brine: salt at ~1% of the breast's weight (e.g. 2 g on a 200 g breast), rest uncovered on a rack in the fridge 1–24 h. Or wet-brine in ~5–6% salt water for 30–60 min. Why: salt dissolves muscle proteins so they hold water and season through the whole thickness, not just the crust.
  2. Prep — even thickness. Gently pound or butterfly the thick end so the breast is uniform. Why: a tapered breast means the thin end hits the cliff while the thick end is still raw. Even geometry = even doneness.
  3. Execute — moderate heat. Sear one side to colour, flip, then drop to medium-low. Gentle heat widens your margin because the temperature gradient from surface to core is shallower. Why: a screaming pan overshoots the core before the outside is done; a moderate pan lets the centre catch up under control.
  4. Execute — pull early. Probe the thickest point. Pull at 60–62 °C / 140–144 °F. Why: carryover will carry it into 63–65 °C, and a hold at 60 °C+ is pasteurising as it rests.
  5. Evaluate & correct. Rest 5 min, slice against the grain. Juicy and opaque = success. Overshot and stringy = pull 3 °C earlier next time and confirm your carryover. Pink and translucent at the core = under; return briefly and re-probe.
L3 Optimization

Crispy-skin chicken + the pan sauce off the fond

  1. Prep — dry the skin. Dry-brine uncovered in the fridge overnight. Why: crisp skin is a dehydration problem. Water on the skin costs energy to boil off before any browning — and while it boils, the skin can't exceed 100 °C or brown. Dry skin browns immediately.
  2. Execute — cold-start render. Skin-side down into a cold or barely warm pan, then bring up the heat. Press flat with a spatula for the first minute for full contact. Why: a slow ramp renders the fat layer out gradually instead of seizing the skin; rendered fat is what fries it crisp.
  3. Execute — don't move it. 8–12 min skin-side down until deep gold and rigid. Flip only to finish the flesh. Why: contact + patience = even crust.
  4. Execute — build the sauce. Remove chicken to rest. Pour off excess fat, leaving the browned fond. Deglaze with wine/stock, scrape every brown speck loose, reduce, then mount off-heat with cold butter (or add a splash of cream) to gloss and thicken. Why: fond is concentrated Maillard flavour — soluble in liquid, which is what deglazing captures.
  5. Evaluate. Skin shatters; sauce coats the back of a spoon and tastes of the chicken, not just wine.
  6. Correct. Broken/greasy sauce → too hot when mounting butter: pull off heat, whisk in a spoon of cold stock to re-emulsify. Bitter sauce → fond scorched black: next time deglaze sooner.
L4 Mastery

Roast whole bird + stock from the carcass

  1. Prep. Dry-brine the whole bird 1–2 days, uncovered in the fridge. Consider spatchcocking (remove backbone, flatten) so breast and thigh finish closer together. Why: the whole-bird problem is that breast (63 °C) and thigh (74 °C+) want different targets on the same animal. Flattening + cooler breast placement narrows the gap.
  2. Execute. Roast hot enough to brown skin; pull when breast reads 62 °C / 144 °F and thigh reads 74 °C+ / 165 °F+. Rest 15–20 min. Why: carryover finishes the breast during a long rest without cooking it to death.
  3. Execute — stock. Carcass + aromatics (onion, carrot, celery, bay) covered in cold water. Bring to a bare quiver — never a rolling boil — and hold 3–6 h, skimming. Why: a gentle simmer extracts collagen into gelatin for body; a hard boil emulsifies fat into the water and agitates particles into cloud. Clear stock = gentle stock.
  4. Evaluate. Bird: juicy breast, gelatinous thigh, crisp skin. Stock: clear, savoury, sets to a soft jelly when chilled (proof of extracted gelatin).
  5. Correct. Greasy/cloudy stock → it boiled: strain, chill, lift the fat cap, and next time keep it below a simmer. Weak, watery stock → too little collagen or too short: use more joints/wings and go longer.
06

Eggs: the same curve, faster and less forgiving

Everything above was chicken. Now spend the model on eggs — because an egg is the same protein-on-a-temperature-curve problem compressed into a smaller, faster, more punishing window. A chicken breast gives you a few degrees of slack and a minute to react. An egg gives you seconds. That is exactly why it is the best daily rep you own: it exposes a heat-control error immediately, cheaply, and in a form you can eat while you learn. Master the egg and the breast feels forgiving.

The variable is still internal temperature

White and yolk are two different proteins that set at different temperatures — which is the whole reason a runny-yolk, set-white egg is even possible. Cook to the window between them and hold there. Blow past it and the yolk turns chalky and the white turns rubber. There is no "egg is done" number; there is a range you park inside.

Egg protein set-points (why runny yolk / set white works)
White · begins~63 °C / 145 °F — turns from clear to milky, still loose
White · set~65 °C / 149 °F — tender and just firm (some proteins hold out to 80 °C)
Yolk · thickens~65 °C / 149 °F — starts to gel
Yolk · jammy~68 °C / 154 °F — fudgy, spoonable — the sweet spot
Yolk · firm~70 °C / 158 °F — crumbly, past runny
Whole beaten egg / custardsets ~73–80 °C / 163–176 °F — pull scrambles & hollandaise well below this

The four dishes below are not four recipes — they are four settings on the same dial, arranged from "gentle heat, keep it moving" to "gentle heat, barely touch it." What good looks like, how to get there, and the one error that ruins each:

French omelette

What good looks like: pale yellow, no browning, rolled into a soft cigar, still creamy (baveuse) inside. Technique: low heat, salted beaten eggs into foaming butter, then constant stirring/shaking to build tiny curds; stop while the top is still wet, tilt, and roll — it finishes on carryover off the heat. Common mistake: pan too hot. Colour and rubber are the same error the breast taught you — overshoot.

Omurice

What good looks like: a silky, un-browned egg blanket draped over ketchup fried rice, set but glossy. Technique: it is the French-omelette skill on a bigger canvas — soft-scramble a thin egg layer, then roll or drape it over the rice while the surface is still barely set. Common mistake: cooking the egg to dry, opaque, squeaky — the same overshoot, more surface area to get wrong.

Eggs Benedict

What good looks like: poached eggs with set whites and liquid yolks, under a warm, smooth, unbroken hollandaise. Technique: poach fresh eggs in barely-quivering water (a splash of vinegar helps the white gather); hollandaise is a warm yolk-and-butter emulsion — whisk over gentle heat and keep it below ~73 °C / 163 °F. Common mistake: hollandaise too hot → the yolk scrambles and the sauce breaks. It is the breast's overshoot at emulsion scale.

Shakshuka

What good looks like: eggs nestled in simmering tomato with whites just set and yolks still runny. Technique: crack eggs into wells in a gently bubbling sauce, cover to trap heat, and cook on low — the sauce, not a screaming pan, is your heat source; pull while the yolks jiggle. Common mistake: sauce too hot or left too long → rubbery yolks. Residual heat keeps cooking after the lid comes off, so pull early.

Controlled experiment · E-egg
Goal
Feel the curve in the most sensitive protein you have.
Keep constant
Two eggs from the same carton, same pan, same butter, same salt.
Change
Heat only. Scramble one on the lowest flame with constant stirring; scramble the other on medium-high.
Expected
Low heat → small, glossy, creamy curds. High heat → large, dry, weeping curds in half the time.
Observe
How little heat it takes, and how the moment of "just set → overcooked" arrives in seconds, not minutes. That interval is the whole skill.
Mediocre → Excellent

Cook eggs daily. They are the cheapest, fastest feedback loop in the kitchen — one egg, one variable, thirty seconds to a verdict. The cook who makes a flawless French omelette is not gifted; they have simply run this rep a few hundred times and can now read the pan the way you read a shot pulling long.

07

Beginner mistakes & troubleshooting

Chicken breast is dry Fault
Causes
Cooked past ~68 °C; cooked to time not temperature; ignored carryover; unbrined lean meat; tapered thickness overcooking the thin end.
Diagnose
Probe the finished centre. Reading 70 °C+ confirms overshoot. Juice pooling on the board = fibres wrung out and no rest.
Fix
Dry-brine at 1% weight; even out thickness; cook on moderate heat; pull at 60–62 °C; rest 5 min. Next attempt, pull 3 °C earlier than felt "safe" and let carryover finish it.
Thigh is rubbery / chewy Fault
Causes
Under-cooked — pulled around 63–68 °C as if it were a breast. The collagen never melted.
Diagnose
Springy, tight texture; connective tissue still tough. Probe likely below 72 °C.
Fix
Cook it more, to 74–88 °C. Thighs get better with heat, not worse. This is the one cut where "cook it longer" is the correct instinct.
Skin is soft / soggy, not crisp Fault
Causes
Wet skin; crowded pan (steam); pan too cool to render; flipping too early; resting skin-down on a plate that traps steam.
Diagnose
Pale, flabby, greasy skin. Fat still visible under it = under-rendered.
Fix
Dry-brine uncovered overnight; cold-start and render slowly, pressing flat; give each piece space; rest skin-up on a rack.
Stock is cloudy, greasy, or weak Fault
Causes
Hard boil emulsified the fat and stirred up particles; too little connective tissue; simmered too short; never skimmed.
Diagnose
Milky liquid, fat slick, thin flavour, doesn't gel when chilled.
Fix
Keep it at a bare quiver, skim scum, use joints/wings/feet for gelatin, go 3–6 h, strain through muslin, chill and lift the fat.
Hollandaise broke / turned to scrambled egg Fault
Causes
Too hot — yolk proteins passed ~70 °C and curdled instead of emulsifying; or butter added too fast for the lecithin to hold it.
Diagnose
Grainy, split, or a sweaty oil layer. Warm ≠ hot: the bowl should never be too hot to hold.
Fix
Work over gentle heat/off the heat in bursts; whisk butter in slowly. To rescue a split sauce, whisk a fresh yolk (or a spoon of warm water) in a clean bowl and dribble the broken sauce back in.
French omelette is browned / rubbery Fault
Causes
Heat too high; not agitating; cooked too long. Browning means the surface blew past the gentle set point.
Diagnose
Golden/tan exterior, firm throughout. A French omelette should be pale yellow and creamy (baveuse) inside.
Fix
Low heat, constant stirring/shaking for small curds, then stop while the top is still glossy and just-wet. Residual heat finishes it as you roll.
08

Controlled experiments

Change one variable, hold the rest, taste side by side. This is how the model stops being something you read and becomes something you know.

Controlled experiment · E1 — the cliff, tasted
Goal
Feel the 63 °C vs 74 °C difference in your own mouth.
Keep constant
Two breasts, same bird, same thickness (pound to match), same seasoning, same pan, cooked together.
Change
Pull one at 63 °C / 145 °F, the other at 74 °C / 165 °F. Rest both equally.
Expected
The 63 °C piece is markedly juicier and more tender; the 74 °C piece is firmer, paler-juiced, chalkier.
Observe
Both are safe. The difference is pure moisture. This single tasting kills the "165 or bust" myth for good.
Controlled experiment · E2 — brine vs no brine
Goal
Quantify what salt actually does.
Keep constant
Two equal breasts, same final temp (pull both at 62 °C), same rest.
Change
One dry-brined at 1% weight for 12 h; the other salted only at the pan.
Expected
Brined piece is juicier, seasoned to the centre, and more forgiving if you overshoot slightly.
Observe
Weigh both before and after cooking — the brined one loses a smaller percentage of its weight. That lost weight is the water you taste as dryness.
Controlled experiment · E3 — skin dried overnight vs patted dry
Goal
Prove that crisp skin is a moisture problem.
Keep constant
Two thighs, same pan, same heat, same render time and technique.
Change
One dry-brined uncovered in the fridge overnight; the other just patted with towel at the pan.
Expected
The overnight-dried skin browns faster and shatters; the patted one is a shade paler and chewier.
Observe
Same heat, same time — only surface water differed. That's the whole lesson of browning in one bite.
Controlled experiment · E4 — measure your carryover
Goal
Learn your pan and cut's carryover so you can pull by prediction.
Keep constant
One breast, one probe left in it.
Change
Nothing — just pull at 60 °C and record the temperature every 30 s while it rests.
Expected
The core keeps climbing, peaks 3–6 °C higher, then falls.
Observe
That peak number is your carryover. From now on, pull at (target − carryover). This one measurement upgrades every cook after it.

Practice system

Repetition with feedback, not repetition alone. Each drill has a pass/fail you can measure so you're training the model, not just making dinner.

Practice drill · L1

Probe-and-pull breast

Goal
Hit a juicy breast three times in a row.
Difficulty
Beginner
Materials
Breast, salt, probe, moderate pan.
Steps
Even the thickness, moderate heat, probe often, pull at 60–62 °C, rest, slice, taste.
Success
Opaque, juicy, no translucent core; juice stays in the meat.
Failure modes
Dry (overshot / no rest); pink core (under); grey outside (pan too hot / wet meat).
Improve
Log pull temp and result each time; converge on your carryover.

Beginner–Intermediate

Skin-on thighs, rack, cast-iron/stainless pan.

Dry-brine uncovered overnight, cold-start skin-down, press, don't move 8–12 min, finish flesh to 74 °C+.

Deep-gold rigid skin that cracks; no flabby patches; flesh silky.

Practice drill · L3

Deglaze-to-sauce reps

Goal
Turn fond into a glossy, balanced pan sauce in under 5 minutes.
Difficulty
Intermediate
Materials
Fond in the pan, stock/wine, cold butter, acid (lemon/vinegar).
Steps
Pour off fat, deglaze, scrape, reduce by half, mount butter off-heat, adjust salt + acid.

Practice drill · L4 — the egg thermometer

Daily French omelette

Goal
A pale, creamy, un-browned rolled omelette — the finest test of low-heat control you own.
Difficulty
Advanced (deceptively)
Materials
2–3 eggs, butter, non-stick/carbon pan, fork/spatula.
Steps
Low heat, constant agitation for small curds, stop while top is glossy-wet, roll, finish on residual heat.
Success
No colour anywhere; soft, custardy interior (baveuse); smooth surface.
Failure modes
Browned (too hot); rubbery (overcooked); torn (moved too fast / bad pan).
Improve
Lower the heat until you're almost impatient. That's the setting. One a day for two weeks.

Projects

Crispy-chicken sandwich

Juicy interior, crunchy outside, seasoned to the centre; bread not soggy.

Juicy chicken, glossy sauce that tastes of the pan, everything hot at once.

Project · Advanced — eggs

Eggs Benedict

Objective
Poached eggs + hollandaise: the graduation test of gentle egg-protein control.
Skills trained
Poaching (fresh eggs, vinegar, vortex), warm yolk emulsion (lecithin), holding a sauce warm without breaking it, plating hot.
Expected result
Runny-yolk poached eggs; smooth, warm, unbroken hollandaise; assembled hot.
Evaluation
Hollandaise never curdled or split; poached whites set, yolks liquid. If it broke, you ran too hot — the same lesson as the breast, at egg scale.
Why it works — the whole module in one line

Chicken breast, thigh, stock, omelette, hollandaise, poached egg — every one is the same problem: a protein denaturing on a temperature curve, and your job is to stop it at the right point. Eggs are just the fastest, most sensitive version. If you can control an egg, you can control anything with a pulse.

Assessment

Knowledge check — understanding, not recall

Two chicken breasts are cooked to exactly the same final internal temperature but end up with noticeably different textures. Give two variables that could cause that. (Consider: was one brined? did the heat ramp differ, changing the surface-to-core gradient? was one rested and the other sliced immediately?)

A thigh pulled at 65 °C is rubbery, but a breast pulled at 65 °C is perfect. Explain why the same number is right for one cut and wrong for the other, using the words collagen and gelatin.

Why can chicken be perfectly safe at 60 °C / 140 °F even though the "official" number is 74 °C / 165 °F? What is the missing variable that makes both true?

Your hollandaise broke and your French omelette browned. Name the single underlying error both share, and explain why an egg exposes it faster than a chicken breast does.

You pull a breast at 64 °C and it comes out overcooked. Nothing was wrong with your target. What did you fail to account for, and how would you measure it once so it never happens again?

Practical challenge

The blind carryover cook. Take one breast. Predict, before cooking, the exact temperature you'll pull it at to land in the 63–65 °C window after resting — based on your measured carryover from E4. Cook it, pull at your predicted number, and probe the peak during the rest. You pass if the rested core lands between 63 and 65 °C and the meat is visibly juicy. This proves you're cooking by model, not by luck.

Reflection

After a week of reps, write down three things: (1) your pan's measured carryover on your usual breast thickness; (2) the one variable that changed your results the most (brine? heat level? pull temp? rest?); and (3) which egg dish exposed your heat control as still too aggressive. If you can answer all three with numbers, you've moved from following recipes to running the system — which was the entire point.
